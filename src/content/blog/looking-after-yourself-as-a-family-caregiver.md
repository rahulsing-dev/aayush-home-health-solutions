In many Indian families, caring for our parents is simply what we do. It is how we were raised, and for many of us it is a privilege. But love does not make the work any lighter. Helping a parent bathe, managing medicines, sitting up through difficult nights and dealing with doctors, all alongside a job and your own family, can slowly wear down even the most devoted son or daughter.

Our founder knows this first-hand. Aayush began after he went through the struggle of balancing his career with caring for his father, who had cancer. These are the things we would want every family caregiver to know.

## Recognise the signs of strain

Caregiver strain builds up slowly, and it is easy to dismiss as "just being tired". Watch for:

- feeling exhausted most of the time, even after rest
- trouble sleeping, or sleeping much more than usual
- feeling irritable, impatient or resentful, and then guilty about it
- losing interest in things you used to enjoy, or pulling away from friends
- frequent headaches or body aches, or falling ill more often
- putting off your own health check-ups and medicines
- feeling that no one else can do it properly, so you never take a break

If several of these sound familiar, it is a sign that something needs to change, not a sign that you are failing.

## Share the work, specifically

"Let me know if you need anything" is kindly meant, but it rarely leads anywhere. It works better to be specific. Sit down with your siblings and other relatives, write down everything that caring involves and divide it up: who handles hospital visits, who manages medicines and refills, who covers weekends, and who looks after the paperwork and payments.

Family members who live in another city can still help. They can book appointments, order medicines, handle bills or simply call your parent every evening, which gives you a break and gives your parent something to look forward to.

## Take breaks before you need them

Regular breaks are not a luxury. They are what keep you able to care. A short walk every day, an evening with friends or one full night's sleep a week can make a real difference. Plan breaks in advance, with someone lined up to take over, rather than waiting until you are at breaking point.

## Look after your own health

Caregivers often put their own health last. Keep your own doctor's appointments, take your own medicines, eat proper meals and try to stay active.

If you help your parent move, get out of bed or use the toilet, ask a nurse or physiotherapist to show you how to do it safely. The right technique protects your back, and your parent will feel more secure too.

## Talk about how you feel

Grief, frustration, guilt and loneliness are common feelings for caregivers, especially when a parent's personality or abilities change. Talking to a friend, a family member, a counsellor or a support group can help. If you feel low or hopeless for more than a couple of weeks, speak to a doctor.

## Know when to bring in help

There may come a point when your parent needs more care than the family can give, or care that needs training, such as nursing after a hospital stay or support for advanced dementia. Arranging professional help is not handing over your responsibility. It is a way of making sure your parent is well looked after, and of letting you be their son or daughter again, not only their caregiver.

Professional support can be arranged for a fixed period, for example while a parent recovers from an illness, or on a continuing basis.

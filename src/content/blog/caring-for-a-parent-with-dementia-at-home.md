When a parent is diagnosed with dementia, families often feel two things at once: a wish to keep them at home, among familiar people and things, and a worry about how everyone will manage.

Dementia is a general term for conditions that affect memory, thinking and everyday functioning. Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause. It usually progresses over time, so the support your parent needs will change, and what works one day may not work the next. The ideas below are a good place to start.

## Keep the day predictable

A steady routine is reassuring when memory is unreliable. Try to keep waking up, meals, bathing, walks and bedtime at roughly the same times each day, and in the same order. Keep familiar objects in their usual places.

A large clock and a calendar showing the day and date, placed where your parent can see them, can help them stay oriented.

Plan the harder tasks, such as bathing or a visit to the doctor, for the time of day when your parent is usually at their best. For many people that is the morning. Some people with dementia become more restless or confused in the late afternoon and evening. If that happens, a calm, well-lit room, less noise and a quiet, familiar activity around that time can help.

## Communicate with patience

- Approach from the front, make eye contact and say who you are if needed.
- Speak slowly and clearly, in short sentences. Ask one question at a time.
- Offer a choice between two things rather than an open question: "Would you like tea or coffee?" is easier than "What would you like?"
- Give plenty of time for an answer. If you need to repeat a question, use the same words.
- Break tasks into small steps and offer one step at a time.
- Avoid arguing or correcting. It often helps more to respond to the feeling behind what your parent says than to the facts. If they are anxious about "going home", for example, comfort and a change of activity may work better than explaining that they are already there.
- Pay attention to tone and body language. A gentle voice and a calm face often say more than words.

## Make the home safer

As dementia progresses, judgement and awareness of danger can be affected. A few changes reduce the risks:

- Keep medicines, cleaning products, knives and sharp tools locked away or out of sight.
- When your parent cannot be supervised in the kitchen, turn off the gas at the cylinder regulator.
- Follow the basics of [preventing falls at home](/blog/preventing-falls-at-home-for-elderly-parents): good lighting, clear floors, grab bars and non-slip mats in the bathroom.
- Simple labels or pictures on doors and cupboards, such as on the bathroom door, can help your parent find their way.
- If your parent tends to wander, make sure they carry identification with a family phone number, keep a recent photograph handy, and let trusted neighbours and your building's security staff know.

## Help them stay involved

Doing things, rather than having everything done for them, can help people with dementia keep a sense of purpose and independence. Look for activities your parent still enjoys, even if they are done slowly or imperfectly: folding clothes, sorting vegetables, watering plants, listening to old songs, looking through family albums or going for a short walk. Praise the effort, not the result.

## Keep the doctor involved

Regular check-ups matter. Tell the doctor about changes in memory, mood, sleep, appetite or behaviour, particularly sudden ones. A sudden increase in confusion can have other causes, such as an infection or a side effect of a medicine, that need prompt attention. Keep an up-to-date list of medicines and take it to every appointment.

## Look after the family, too

Caring for someone with dementia is demanding, emotionally as well as physically, and it often goes on for years. Share responsibilities among family members where you can, take regular breaks and accept help when it is offered. Talking to others who care for someone with dementia, or to a counsellor, can help as well.
